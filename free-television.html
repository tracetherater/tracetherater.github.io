Should Television Be Free? My Vision for the Future of TV

By Trace Thompkins | Trace The Rater
November 13, 2026

Watch the video · Read the article

Watch: My vision for free television

About this companion article: This piece expands on my November 13 video script; it is not a word-for-word transcript. Advertising and subscriptions are not inherently double charging. My focus is the value viewers receive and the choices available to them.

Television should have a free, reliable foundation: entertainment people can enjoy without another monthly subscription, supported by advertising worth some of their attention. Alongside it, there should be paid choices for viewers who want more.

DING DING DING! It’s Trace The Rater time! In my November 13 video script, I asked a simple question: Should television be free? My answer starts with access, but it doesn’t end there. A free service should still care about the experience: imaginative commercials, dependable playback, clear sound, and a picture that respects the original work.

A free foundation, with more than one way to watch

I support over-the-air television. There is value in turning on a television and finding something to watch without signing up for another recurring bill. But supporting broadcasting doesn’t mean expecting everyone to install an antenna.

Reception depends on where you live and what your setup can receive. Some people find an antenna convenient; others face unreliable reception or simply prefer an app. Free, authorized streaming and free broadcasting should complement each other. Neither needs to be the only acceptable way to watch.

“Free” also deserves an honest explanation. Equipment can cost money, and streaming still requires an internet connection and a compatible device. A service without a subscription fee does not eliminate every household expense. It can, however, give people another useful choice within the setup they already have.

Bring Saturday-morning effort back to advertising

When I think about the spirit of Saturday-morning television, I think about commercials with personality: catchy jingles, colorful animation, memorable mascots, puppetry, and little stories that felt like miniature productions. That is the level of creative effort I would love to see again, with modern production quality and fresh ideas.

It isn’t a request to repeat the past scene for scene. Give artists, writers, performers, and musicians room to make something surprising. An advertisement can communicate what a product offers and still be funny, visually inventive, or musically memorable.

Creativity also needs restraint. Even a great commercial becomes irritating when it plays endlessly. I want reasonable breaks, less repetition, clear separation between advertising and programming, and honest messages—especially when children are watching. High effort should mean respecting the audience as well as spending money on production.

Ads and subscriptions: judge the value

Paying a subscription and still seeing commercials can feel frustrating. But advertising plus a subscription is not inherently double charging. Producing, licensing, and distributing television costs money, and a service may use both sources of revenue to support its offering. Buying an advertiser’s product does not mean that purchase has paid my personal television bill, either.

The question I care about is what viewers receive in return. Does the price make sense for the programming and features? Is the ad load reasonable? Are the terms clear before someone signs up? A lower-priced plan with ads may be worthwhile for one household, while another would rather pay more to avoid interruptions.

My frustration is with poor value: repetitive interruptions, confusing promises, and an experience that feels worse despite the bill. Free viewing, paid viewing with ads, and premium viewing can all have a place when the differences are transparent and the choice is meaningful.

Crystal-clear quality should preserve the original picture

I want the clearest picture and sound genuinely available for each program. But improving quality should preserve what was made. If a classic cartoon was presented in 4:3, show it in 4:3. Stretching it to fill a widescreen display changes its proportions; cropping it can remove part of the composition.

Side bars on a widescreen television can be the right way to preserve a 4:3 image. The same principle applies to widescreen films and other formats: let the work keep its intended shape. Filling every inch of the display is less important to me than seeing the complete picture. Netflix’s explanation of black bars describes how bars preserve a title’s format and how zooming can trim the image.

A good restoration should reveal detail without erasing the character of the source. Older footage does not need to look artificially new to look good. I would rather have a faithful presentation than aggressive sharpening, unnecessary smoothing, or a format change that alters the creative choices.

Sound deserves equal care: understandable dialogue, clean playback, sensible volume, and audio that stays in sync with the picture. Accessible captions matter, too. Those details shape the experience just as much as a resolution label does.

Reliability belongs in the definition of quality

A sharp image is little comfort if it keeps freezing or disappearing. My ideal television experience works consistently, whether it arrives through an antenna or an internet connection.

There is no promise that either route will work perfectly everywhere. Broadcast reception can vary; streaming depends on the connection and service; televisions and network equipment need power. I want better reception, more dependable delivery, accessible controls, and thoughtful backup planning where practical. Reliability should be something providers keep improving, rather than a problem viewers are left to work around.

Paid choices should add something viewers want

I am not asking for every program and feature to become free. Paid options can make sense for additional entertainment, fewer advertisements, downloads, or other benefits a viewer actually uses. What matters is being clear about what is included and letting people decide whether it is worth the cost.

For me, the future should offer a strong free foundation alongside optional paid services. Someone who enjoys broadcast television should feel welcome. Someone who prefers free streaming should feel welcome. Someone who chooses premium entertainment should feel welcome, too.

My vision is free access where possible, creative advertising, dependable picture and sound, original presentation, and freedom of choice. Technology should make watching television more enjoyable and accessible. That is the standard I want broadcasters, streaming services, and advertisers to work toward.

What would you choose?

Would you prefer free television with creative commercials, a paid premium experience, or a mix of both? And what matters most to you: the price, the programming, fewer interruptions, or faithful picture and sound?
